## Gifts

A gift is bought by one customer and redeemed by another. It may be a
subscription, an add-on, or wallet credit.

### Whose history it appears in

The order belongs to the sender and is never copied into the recipient's
history. `orders.user_id` is the sender; the entitlement or ledger entry the
claim produces carries the recipient. That is what keeps a gift out of the
recipient's payment history while still giving them what was bought.

### Claim codes

A code is sixteen Crockford base-32 characters — no `I`, `L`, `O`, or `U`, so
nothing in it is visually ambiguous and no accidental word appears in it. Eighty
bits, combined with the attempt ceiling and the endpoint's rate limit, makes
guessing one impractical.

Only the SHA-256 of the code is stored. A database read never yields a
redeemable code. The last four characters are kept separately as a hint, which
is enough for a sender to tell two of their own gifts apart and for support to
confirm which gift a conversation is about, and not enough to redeem one.

Input is normalised before lookup: separators are stripped and the three
Crockford substitutions (`I`/`L` to `1`, `O` to `0`) are applied, because those
are the characters a person copying by hand reliably confuses. Anything else is
rejected rather than guessed at.

### Redemption rules

Redemption is exactly once by construction: the claim statement only matches a
gift that is deliverable and unexpired, so a second claim of the same code
updates no row.

The refusal reasons are checked in a deliberate order:

1. **Attempt ceiling.** After ten failed presentations a code stops answering
   anything at all, rather than continuing to leak which failure it is. The
   count is durable state on the row, so it survives a restart and cannot be
   reset by waiting out a rate-limit window.
2. **Status.** A gift whose payment has not settled answers "not yet" rather
   than a refusal. A claimed, revoked, refunded, or expired gift is refused.
3. **Expiry**, even if the sweeper has not run yet.
4. **Self-claim.** A sender cannot redeem their own gift: routing money through
   the gift path would otherwise reach a promotion or reward they are not
   eligible for.
5. **Named recipient.** A gift addressed to a Telegram identifier is claimable
   only by that identifier.

### Revocation and refunds

An operator may revoke a gift that is pending, deliverable, or expired. A
claimed gift is deliberately **not** revocable: the recipient already holds what
it bought, and taking that back is a refund decision made against their
entitlement or ledger, with its own permission and its own record.

Only a gift that was never redeemed is refundable to its sender. Refunding a
claimed gift would mean the sender's money back and the recipient's entitlement
intact, which is exactly the abuse this rule prevents.

Revoking takes a row lock first, so a revoke and a claim arriving at the same
moment serialise: whichever commits first wins and the other sees a state its
guarded update refuses.

## Digital goods

A digital good is not VPN access. Nothing in this area creates, modifies, reads,
or depends on a Remnawave entitlement, and a delivery failure here can never
disturb a subscription.

### The gateway, and why Omniflow does not talk to Fragment

Fragment has no API. Buying there means driving an authenticated browser
session through a stateful multi-step flow and settling the result with an
on-chain TON transfer from a wallet derived from a mnemonic.

Omniflow deliberately does not hold that wallet. A self-hosted product should
not ship a configuration field that can spend real money, and a stateful
scraping session is not something a background worker can share safely. Instead
the operator runs a **gateway** that owns the Fragment session, the TON wallet,
and the settlement, and Omniflow calls it over HTTP Basic as the fixed
`omniflow` user.

The adapter interface stays small — supports, quote, deliver, poll, balance —
so a second gateway or a different goods provider is five methods, not a
rewrite of the shop.

### What the gateway does and does not offer

| Capability | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Deliver Stars by username | `POST /admin/stars/send`, minimum 50 |
| Deliver Premium by username | `POST /admin/premium/send`, 3, 6, or 12 months |
| Star cost | `GET /admin/stars/value`, a per-star price in the gateway's currency |
| Premium cost | Not published — the operator sets the sale price in the panel |
| Idempotency key | **Not honoured** |
| Poll a submission | Not offered |
| Wallet balance | Not offered |
| Validate a recipient before payment | Not offered |

Delivery is addressed by username. The `user_id` the gateway also takes is
bookkeeping for its own customer records, so Omniflow sends the **buyer's**
Telegram identifier — it does not know the numeric identifier behind an
arbitrary handle, and the handle is what actually receives the goods.

<Warning>
  **Two gateway limitations affect what Omniflow can promise.**

  There is no idempotency key, so a request whose answer never arrives is
  genuinely ambiguous: the goods may already have been sent and the operator's
  funds already spent.

  And a success is not proof of delivery. The gateway answers 200 for a purchase
  its own processor abandoned — insufficient TON, an expired Fragment session,
  and a rejected purchase are reported to an operator chat and then returned
  from normally. Until the gateway distinguishes those, an accepted submission
  means "the gateway took it".

  Both are fixed on the gateway side: honour an `Idempotency-Key` header, and
  raise on a failed purchase instead of returning. Until then, read "delivered"
  in the panel as "submitted and accepted".
</Warning>

### Pricing

What the customer pays is derived from what the provider charges: a markup in
basis points, then a rounding rule, both operator-configured per product.

| Rounding | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| `none`, `up_minor` | The exact amount |
| `up_unit` | Up to a whole major unit |
| `up_ten_units` | Up to a multiple of ten major units |
| `up_hundred_units` | Up to a multiple of a hundred major units |

Every mode rounds up, and the markup itself rounds up rather than truncating. A
shop that loses a minor unit on every sale is a shop whose configured margin is
not the margin it earns.

Stars are quoted from the gateway's published per-star value, rounded to the
minor unit per star and then multiplied — the same arithmetic the gateway
charges against.

Premium has no published cost. The operator sets the sale price directly with
`fixed_amount_minor`, and Omniflow records that the cost is unknown rather than
zero, so the finance view reports the margin as unknown instead of as a hundred
per cent.

Both sides of a quote are stored on the order: what the provider asked and what
the customer was shown. Their difference is the realised margin, which is why
the finance view can report it without recomputing a markup that may since have
changed. Because a gateway rate can move, a quote carries an expiry;
`quote_ttl_seconds` sets it, and a stale quote produces a new one rather than a
charge at the old number.

### Recipients

The recipient is a Telegram username and nothing else. No display name, no
numeric identifier, no profile data: delivery needs the username and support
needs to be able to answer "where did it go", and neither needs more.

Input is normalised from the forms people actually paste — a bare handle, an
`@` prefix, a `t.me/` link, a link with a query string — and validated against
Telegram's own rule of five to thirty-two characters beginning with a letter.
The gateway exposes no pre-payment check, so a handle that is well-formed but
unreachable is caught at delivery and refunded.

### Delivery and failure classification

One delivery row per order, enforced by the primary key. That constraint stops a
replayed job, a duplicated webhook, or a retried worker from submitting a second
time. It cannot undo a purchase that already happened, which is exactly why the
ambiguous class below exists.

A failure is classified, and the class decides what happens next:

| Class | Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| `retryable` | Retried on the backoff schedule |
| `provider_balance` | Retried, and raises an alert — the operator can top up |
| `provider_unavailable` | Retried; an outage or a rejected credential, not a rejection of the order |
| `recipient_invalid` | Refunded — nothing the worker does will make the username resolve |
| `permanent` | Refunded |
| `ambiguous` | **Neither.** Parked for an operator |

Every class resolves in exactly one way, which is asserted by a unit test.
Retrying an ambiguous purchase could deliver and charge twice; refunding one
could give money back for goods the recipient received. Both answers can be
wrong in a way that costs somebody money, so the delivery stops and waits.

A server error from the gateway is ambiguous rather than retryable, because the
gateway settles on-chain and writes its own records afterwards — a 5xx can be
raised from code that runs after the goods were paid for. A lost connection is
ambiguous for the same reason.

Backoff doubles from a minute to a ceiling of an hour, and six attempts — a
little over two hours — is where a retryable failure becomes a refund. The
refund is an ordinary wallet credit, so it appears in the customer's wallet
history exactly like any other.

### Paying with wallet balance

A shop purchase is an ordinary order, so wallet-first payment applies to it
unchanged: the balance covers what it can and the remainder becomes the external
amount a provider settles. A customer with enough balance pays for Premium or
Stars without a payment provider being involved at all.

### Spend controls

The provider record carries a low-balance threshold and a rolling spend ceiling
measured in provider cost rather than customer price — the ceiling protects the
operator's funding source, and that is what the provider actually draws down. A
zero ceiling means no ceiling.

The gateway publishes no balance, so the low-balance alert has nothing to fire
on for it today; an exhausted TON wallet instead surfaces as deliveries the
gateway accepts and silently abandons. Exposing a balance endpoint is the single
change that would fix that.

Credentials — the gateway URL and its shared token — are sealed with
`APP_DATA_ENCRYPTION_KEY`, never returned by the API after they are written, and
excluded from diagnostics.
