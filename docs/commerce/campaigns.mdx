## A segment is filters, never stored SQL

An audience segment is a set of named filters compiled to parameterised SQL at
read time. It is deliberately not a saved query fragment, for two reasons that
each outweigh the flexibility a stored fragment would buy.

An operator has to be able to read what a segment selects before sending
anything to it, and an auditor has to be able to check afterwards what it
selected. Neither is possible if the definition is a query somebody wrote once
and nobody can now interpret.

And a stored fragment is an injection surface with an audit trail attached.
Every value here reaches SQL as a parameter, and the vocabulary is closed, so a
segment cannot express anything the vocabulary does not allow — including
reading a table it has no business reading.

### The vocabulary

| Filter | Selects |
| --- | --- |
| `status` | Account status |
| `locale` | Interface language |
| `subscription` | `active`, `expired`, or `none` |
| `expiringWithinDays` | Live subscriptions ending soon |
| `inactiveForDays` | No paid order in that many days |
| `minimumSpendMinor` | Lifetime settled spend |
| `loyaltyTier` | Current standing |
| `planCode` | Holding a given plan |

A key outside this list is refused on write rather than ignored. Silently
dropping a filter would send to a wider audience than the operator asked for.

### It explains itself

Every segment carries a generated explanation of what it selects, in the same
order as the filters. It is generated from the filters rather than typed by the
operator, so it cannot describe something the query does not do.

Compilation is deterministic: the same definition always produces the same SQL
and the same explanation. A segment whose rendering changes between reviews is
one nobody can approve.

<Warning>
  **An empty segment is every customer.** That is legitimate — an outage notice
  means everybody — and the segment says so explicitly rather than rendering as
  a blank definition. Nobody should mail the whole base by forgetting to add a
  filter.
</Warning>

## The news lifecycle

A publication timestamp alone cannot express "written but not ready",
"scheduled for Tuesday", or "taken down because it was wrong" — and the last is
the one an operator needs most urgently.

Posts move through `draft` → `scheduled` → `published`, and from published to
`unpublished` or `archived`. `published_at` is set once and kept, so
unpublishing and republishing does not rewrite when customers first saw it.

Scheduled posts are published by the worker on time, rather than depending on
somebody being at a keyboard.

## Templates validate their own variables

A template declares the variables its body may use. A body referencing an
undeclared variable is refused on write.

The alternative is a customer receiving "Hello {{name}}" because nobody checked,
and by then it has gone to everybody.

Both languages are required, for the same reason canned support replies require
both: the language of the customer is not the language of the operator.

## How a campaign runs

Creating a campaign queues nothing. The audience is estimated and stored, and
the campaign sits in `draft` — a draft that had already queued its recipients
would make the review step a formality.

The estimate is counted with the same compiled fragment the send will walk, so
what an operator approves and what actually receives the message cannot come
from different definitions.

Once running, the worker expands the audience into per-recipient rows in bounded
batches. The recipient row's primary key is the deduplication: a
paused-and-resumed campaign continues rather than restarting, and a customer
cannot be queued twice.

### Pausing works

Only `running` campaigns are read for delivery. Pausing stops the send at the
next batch without cancelling what has already gone and without losing the
record of who received it.

The permitted transitions are enforced in the statement rather than in the
caller. A campaign that could jump from draft to completed would report a reach
it never had.

A segment that no longer compiles pauses its campaign rather than running
against an audience nobody can read.

## Who is skipped, and why

The worker owns the audience. The bot's notifier owns delivery, because consent,
quiet hours, frequency caps, and Telegram delivery health already live there —
duplicating them would mean two places that can disagree about whether a
customer may be messaged.

A recipient the policy refuses is recorded as suppressed with a reason, never
silently dropped:

| Reason | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `no_consent` | Marketing consent not given |
| `suppressed` | On the suppression list |
| `frequency_cap` | Already had their marketing allowance this window |
| `quiet_hours` | Inside their configured quiet window |
| `delivery_blocked` | Blocked the bot, or the account is deactivated |
| `no_telegram` | No reachable Telegram identity |

An operator reviewing reach needs to see that four hundred people were on the
list and not contacted, and why. A campaign that simply reports a smaller
audience hides the decision.

## Suppression is not the consent flag

They mean different things. Consent is a preference the customer toggles; a
suppression is a standing instruction that survives them toggling it back on by
accident. Suppressions record a reason — customer request, bounce, complaint, or
an operator decision — and who added them.

## What is not stored

Rendered message bodies. The template and its variables are the record, and the
delivery the bot made is what the customer received. Copying the body into a
campaigns table would duplicate customer-facing content into a second retention
regime for no operational gain.
