v0.4 gave a customer one open ticket and an operator a Telegram topic to answer
it in. That is enough for one person answering a handful of tickets a day, and
it stops being enough the moment there are two operators, because nothing says
who is answering what.

## A customer may hold several tickets

The one-open-ticket-per-customer rule is gone. A billing question and a
connection problem are two conversations: forcing them into one thread makes
both harder to answer and impossible to route separately.

The bot still continues the customer's most recent open ticket when they simply
type, because that is what they mean. A second thread is opened deliberately —
from the ticket list in the bot, or by an operator.

## Queues carry the promise

A queue is a named bucket with a first-response target and a resolution target.
The target lives on the queue rather than on the priority because they answer
different questions: priority is how this ticket compares to the work in front
of it, and the target is what the operator promised the customer. A billing
queue may promise four hours for everything in it while a general queue promises
two days.

Exactly one queue is the default, enforced by a partial unique index, so a new
ticket always has somewhere to go. A queue with open tickets in it cannot be
archived — archiving one would hide work rather than finish it.

Every installation starts with a `general` queue. It can be renamed, joined by
others, or demoted from default, but there is always one.

## Internal notes are not messages

A note is a separate table, not a `visibility` flag on `support_messages`.

That is deliberate and it is the whole safety property. Every path that delivers
a message to a customer reads `support_messages`; a flag on that table would
mean every one of those paths has to remember to check it, and the first one
that forgets sends an operator's private note to the customer it is about. A
note cannot be delivered because it is not in the table the delivery path reads.

The panel renders notes and messages in one timeline and styles them
differently, but the reply box and the note box are separate controls with
separate buttons. A toggle would be a state somebody can be wrong about, and
being wrong there means sending the customer a note about them.

## Replies are written here and delivered by the bot

The panel writes the reply; the bot's notifier delivers it. That keeps consent,
quiet hours, Telegram delivery health, and blocked-account handling in one place
instead of duplicated into the panel.

A reply requires an `Idempotency-Key`, which becomes the message's dedupe key,
so a resubmitted form reaches the message that already exists rather than
sending the customer the same answer twice.

The reply body is deliberately absent from the audit metadata. The message
itself is the record; copying it into the audit trail would duplicate customer
content into a second retention regime.

## Canned responses require both languages

A canned reply that exists in one language is one an operator will send to the
wrong half of the customer base, because the language of the customer is not the
language of the operator.

Each response declares the permission needed to send it. A response the operator
may not use is filtered out of the list rather than shown disabled: a list of
replies somebody cannot send is a list they have to read past every time.

## Merging keeps both conversations

The absorbed ticket keeps its row and points at its survivor, and its messages
move so the conversation reads as one thread. Deleting it would lose the
customer's own words and the trail that explains where they went.

A merge across customers is refused. Putting one customer's words into another's
conversation is not a mistake worth warning about afterwards.

## What the report measures, exactly

The definitions travel with the numbers, because a response-time report whose
definition is ambiguous is a report people argue about instead of acting on.

| Measure | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Open tickets | Tickets in the open or pending state, whatever their queue |
| Unassigned | Open or pending tickets nobody has taken |
| Overdue | Open or pending tickets past their queue's first-response target with no operator reply yet. A queue with a zero target never breaches |
| Resolved in window | Tickets that reached resolved or closed inside the window |
| Median first response | Median time from a ticket being created to its first operator reply, over replies made inside the window |
| Replies | Operator messages written by that operator inside the window, including replies to tickets assigned to somebody else |

The response measure is a median rather than a mean. One ticket answered a week
late would otherwise make a good week look bad.

`first_response_at` is set by the first operator reply and never moved, so the
measure survives a conversation that goes back and forth for a week.
`reopened_count` counts the round trips, which is the signal that an answer did
not actually answer the question — a customer who writes back on a resolved
ticket reopens it automatically.

## Permissions

| Action | Permission |
| --- | --- |
| Read queues, tickets, canned replies, and the report | `support.read` |
| Assign, reply, note, tag, merge, and change status | `support.write` |
| Define queues, tags, and canned replies | `settings.write` |

Reading a ticket and answering one are separate because they are separate jobs:
a finance operator investigating a refund needs to read the conversation that led
to it, and should not be able to reply to the customer in the desk's voice.
Defining queues is configuration rather than day-to-day support work, so an
operator answering tickets cannot redefine the promises their queue makes.
