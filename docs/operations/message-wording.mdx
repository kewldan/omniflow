title: Message wording
description: Rewording the transactional notices the installation sends on its own, with a variable reference, a preview, and a test send that never reaches a customer
---

`/admin/settings/notices` changes what the installation says when something
happens to a subscription: it is about to expire, the traffic allowance is
nearly gone, an automatic charge failed.

Campaign copy is written elsewhere, under
[Campaigns](/commerce/campaigns), where you compose a message and choose who
receives it. These are different. They reach every customer, they are triggered
by an event rather than by a decision to send, and they are the voice your
customers hear from you most often — which is why leaving them compiled in was
the wrong default.

## The nine notices

| Notice | Sent when | Values it carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expiry warning | 7, 3, and 1 days before expiry, and on the day | `{days}` |
| Traffic warning | The allowance passes 80%, and again at 100% | `{percent}` |
| Renewal reminder | A subscription is close enough to its end to renew | `{plan}`, `{days}`, `{until}` |
| Grace period | Access continues after the subscription expired | `{until}`, `{graceUntil}` |
| Recovery offer | After the grace period, offering to bring it back | — |
| Activation finished | Provisioning completed and the customer can connect | — |
| Activation delayed | Provisioning is slow enough to deserve an explanation | — |
| Automatic charge failed | A charge failed and will be retried | — |
| Automatic renewal stopped | Dunning gave up | — |

Most carry nothing to substitute. That is not an oversight — the wording an
installation wants to change is usually the tone rather than the data.

## Values are named, not numbered

An override writes `{days}`, not `%d`.

The compiled catalogue uses `fmt.Sprintf`, which is safe for text written beside
the code that renders it and unsafe for text written in a browser. A `%d` that
meets a string prints `%!d(string=Pro)`. A verb too many prints `%!(EXTRA ...)`.
Neither fails — they reach the customer looking broken, and nobody reads the
message between you writing it and them receiving it.

So a placeholder is refused when you save it if the notice does not carry that
value. `{name}` in the expiry warning is an error on the screen you are looking
at, rather than literal braces in ten thousand messages.

## What a save refuses

Everything here would otherwise become a message that fails or looks broken at
the customer, hours later, with nobody watching:

- A value the notice does not carry.
- Markup outside `<b>`, `<i>`, `<u>`, `<s>`, `<code>`, `<pre>`, and `<a>` — the
  subset Telegram's HTML mode accepts.
- A tag that is never closed, or a closing tag that closes nothing.
- A link that is not `https`. A `tg://` or `javascript:` target in a message the
  installation sends on its own initiative is a link the customer has every
  reason to trust and no way to inspect.
- A stray `<`. Telegram refuses a message with one, so "5 < 10" is caught while
  you are still on the screen. Write `&lt;`.
- A blank body, or one over 2000 characters. Telegram accepts 4096; the limit is
  half of it because the notice is prefixed with the subscription line and
  carries a keyboard.

The refusal names the offending placeholder or tag. "Invalid" is not something
anybody can act on while looking at a text area.

## Preview, and test send

**Preview** renders your text against sample values — `{plan}` becomes `Pro`,
`{days}` becomes `5` — so you are judging a message rather than a template. It
renders as plain text in the panel: the body is Telegram markup, this is a
browser, and interpreting it here would show you something neither Telegram nor
the customer produces.

**Send to the operator group** puts one rendered copy into the notice-preview
topic in your operator group. That is what shows the real thing: whether
Telegram accepts the markup, how the emoji look on a phone, where the lines
break.

It never goes to a customer, and that is deliberate. A transactional notice has
a trigger. Manufacturing an expiry warning against a real subscription to see
how it reads would tell somebody their access is ending when it is not.

The body is rendered and stored when you press the button, so the group receives
what was in the editor at that moment — even if you keep editing, and even if
you never save.

## Overrides are exceptions, not copies

A notice with no row for a locale is using the shipped wording. Nothing is
copied into the database when you first open the screen.

This is why **Use the shipped wording** is a delete rather than a write of the
current default. An installation that reverts today and upgrades next month gets
next month's improved wording. Had reverting stored a copy, it would have frozen
that installation on whatever the text happened to be on the day somebody
clicked.

The two languages are independent. Rewriting the English expiry warning decides
nothing about the Russian one, and each is saved on its own request so this
screen cannot overwrite a translation somebody else was working on.

## When it takes effect

On the notifier's next pass, within fifteen minutes.

The wording is read once at the top of a pass and used for every message in it.
That is cheaper than a query per message and more coherent: a hundred expiry
warnings sent together say the same thing, even if you save an edit halfway
through.

If the lookup fails, the notifier keeps whatever was in force rather than
falling back to the shipped text. Changing what every customer reads because one
query timed out would be worse than being one pass out of date.

## Permissions

Reading the wording needs `settings.read`. Writing, reverting, previewing, and
sending a test need `settings.write`.

Preview is a write even though it stores nothing: it runs the renderer over text
the caller supplied, and a read permission should not be a way to do that.

Every save, revert, and test send is in the audit trail as `notice.overridden`,
`notice.reverted`, and `notice.tested`. The body is not in the audit payload —
it is up to 2000 characters of message text readable from the table it was just
written to, and an audit trail records who did what rather than keeping a second
copy of the content.

## Reference

| Route | Method | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `/v1/panel/notices` | `GET` | `settings.read`; every notice with its shipped wording, variables, and overrides |
| `/v1/panel/notices/{code}` | `PUT` | `settings.write`; `{locale, body}`, one locale per request |
| `/v1/panel/notices/{code}` | `DELETE` | `settings.write`; `?locale=` — reverting something untouched is not an error |
| `/v1/panel/notices/{code}/preview` | `POST` | `settings.write`; an empty body previews the shipped wording |
| `/v1/panel/notices/{code}/test` | `POST` | `settings.write`; answers `202`, delivers to the operator group |
| `/v1/panel/notices/{code}/tests` | `GET` | `settings.read`; recent tests and their outcomes |

Refusals are RFC 9457 problem documents with the reason in `detail`.
