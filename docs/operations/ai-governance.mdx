[AI privacy](/operations/ai-privacy) covers what leaves the installation and what
stops it. This page covers the decisions around that: which features are on, who
may use them, what is kept afterwards, and how you find out when the quality
drops.

## Features are enabled one at a time

There are nine features — ticket summaries, suggested replies, rewriting,
translation, classification, marketing drafts, abuse analysis, the admin
copilot, and MCP tools — and each is separately enabled with its own provider,
model, budget, and retention.

A feature is off until you enable it *and* choose a provider and model. A feature
that is switched on but misconfigured shows as "enabled but not usable" rather
than silently behaving as off: a switch that reads as on and behaves as off is
the worst of both.

Every feature has a manual workflow behind it that predates it. Switching AI off
leaves those exactly as they were; the surfaces report "unavailable" rather than
failing.

## What the provider does with your data

Each provider carries your answers from its own terms: whether it retains
requests, whether it may train on them, what region it processes in, and any
retention notice you paste in. Omniflow cannot verify these and does not pretend
to — they are recorded so the panel can warn you **before** a feature is switched
on rather than after data has left.

The warnings render beside the feature's own switch. A warning in a separate
panel is one you read after deciding.

## Usage limits

Ceilings apply at four scopes: one operator, one role, one feature, or the whole
installation. Each bounds requests, tokens, or estimated cost over a window.

When several apply, the **narrowest** one is the one you are told about, because
the remedy differs: "you have used your allowance" and "the installation has used
its allowance" call for different actions from the same person.

<Warning>
  An unreadable meter refuses the call. Spending against a limit nobody can
  measure is how an installation discovers its ceiling from an invoice.
</Warning>

An installation with no limits configured has no ceiling — that is the common
case and a legitimate one.

## Spend and performance

The usage report shows requests, tokens, mean and p95 latency, failures, and
estimated cost, grouped by feature, provider, and model.

No prompt or output content appears in it, because none is stored: the table it
reads has no column for them. A metrics table that *can* hold a ticket body
eventually holds one.

Cost is stated as estimated. Omniflow does not see the invoice, and a column
named `cost_minor` without "estimated" nearby invites somebody to reconcile
against it.

## Retention and legal hold

Retention is set per feature, because the material differs: a marketing draft is
your own copy, and a support summary is somebody's complaint. Prompts are not
kept by default; outputs are, for a period you set.

A legal hold suspends deletion for one subject. It works in one direction only:
it can extend retention of something already kept, and it can never cause
something to be kept that your policy said not to store. A hold is an instruction
not to delete, not an instruction to collect.

## Audit exports identify AI-influenced decisions

When an operator acts on something a model produced, that is recorded with the
subject, the feature, the provider and model, the policy version, and the
operator — and whether they accepted it, **edited** it, or rejected it.

Edited is separate from accepted because rewriting half of a draft is a different
decision from pressing send unchanged. Consequential decisions — the adverse and
financial ones — are flagged, so an export can be narrowed to the ones anybody
will actually ask about.

The export sits behind the audit export permission rather than the settings one:
it is an audit artefact.

## Evaluation and regression thresholds

Omniflow ships evaluation sets for suggested replies, marketing copy, abuse
analysis, and translation. They exist to answer a question you cannot answer by
reading code: **has the behaviour got worse?** A prompt change, a model change,
or a provider change can all degrade an answer without breaking a single test.

Two properties make the sets safe to ship:

**Every case is proven synthetic.** Each case's text runs through the production
redactor at load time, and a case containing anything it would strip is refused.
An evaluation set is exactly the kind of file that accumulates a real ticket
somebody pasted in while debugging, and a promise in a README does not survive
that.

**Every expectation is a property, not a golden string.** "Cites a source it was
given", "stays under the Telegram limit", "does not promise a refund" — these
survive a model that words things differently. A fixture of expected output fails
on the first paraphrase and gets deleted within a month.

### The thresholds

| Dimension | Floor | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Groundedness | 1.0 | An answer built on nothing is not a quality trade-off |
| Citation validity | 1.0 | An invented citation means an invented claim |
| Unsafe advice | 1.0 | Reaching a customer once is once too many |
| Constraints | 1.0 | A broken variable or an over-length message fails the send |
| Refusal | 1.0 | A feature that answers what it should refuse has no floor worth setting |
| Correctness | 0.9 | Language varies |
| Tone | 0.8 | A threshold nobody can hold gets lowered rather than met |

A dimension the suite does not exercise produces **no regression and no
reassurance**. It is reported as untested instead, because conflating "we checked
and it was fine" with "we never checked" is how a gate stops gating.

<Note>
  Evaluation runs are manual today. Running them automatically would spend your
  budget against your configured provider without being asked.
</Note>
